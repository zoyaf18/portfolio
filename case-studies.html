From finding donors to coordinating a response.

BloodNet began as a machine-learning idea and became a product-design problem about conflicting constraints, incomplete information, medical safety, operational handoffs, and human decisions. This is the path between those two versions—not just a tour of the finished UI.

01 / Positioning

Before building another blood app, I had to ask what already existed.

BloodNet wasn't meant to be a new donor directory or a replacement for blood-bank infrastructure. That distinction changed the definition of the product.

Existing systems already address meaningful parts of this space: blood-bank records, donor registries, inventory visibility, and sometimes notifications. e-RaktKosh was an important reference point in the product specification. Treating those functions as completely unsolved would have made for a weak—and misleading—product thesis.

Instead, I framed the opportunity around what happens between discovering supply and resolving a request: comparing options, calculating shortfall, deciding how many donors to contact, obtaining approval, recording what changed, and recalculating the remaining need.

Important existing capabilities

Blood-bank and donor records, inventory lookup, registration, and notification. These are foundations BloodNet should eventually integrate with—not claim to have invented.

The space I chose to work in

Predictive, request-aware coordination: availability → shortage → recommendation → approved action → outcomes. One decision loop rather than another isolated surface.

02 / Framing the problem

The first idea was a ranking model. The real problem was a workflow.

My earliest concept was bounded: given an emergency request and a pool of donors, predict who is most likely to respond successfully and contact those people first.

That is still part of BloodNet. But a high ranking score doesn't answer whether usable blood is already nearby, whether a unit has been reserved for someone else, whether it can reach the hospital in time, who is authorized to act, or what happens after the first attempt fails.

I reframed the objective from “find a compatible donor” to “improve the probability of fulfilling a specific blood request, without unnecessary mobilization or unsafe decisions.”

Initial framing

Inputs: request + donor pool. Output: a prioritized list of likely responders. Useful, but it stops before operational execution.

Expanded framing

Inputs: request, inventory, donor context, urgency, distance, permissions. Output: a reviewable action plan, approved execution, and a continuously updated shortfall.

That shift mattered because optimizing an intermediate metric like “number of matches returned” can look impressive while failing to improve what the hospital actually needs: timely, safe fulfillment.

03 / Experience architecture

One request. Multiple people. A lot of handoffs.

The user experience couldn't be designed as one person's screen. A hospital coordinator initiates the need, a bank operator reviews consequential actions, donors respond when relevant, and regional operators look ahead to emerging shortage pressure.

I separated the roles because they need different information and authority—not merely different dashboard colors.

The pivotal handoff is the one between recommendation and action. Finding potential supply is read-oriented; reserving a unit or contacting someone affects the real world. Those need different controls.

04 / Prioritization

The MVP wasn't a small list of features. It was one complete loop.

The first product milestone needed to demonstrate that a request could become a decision, that a decision could become an approved action, and that the system could observe the result.

That meant prioritizing workflow integrity, not just feature breadth. Some seemingly unglamorous pieces—permissions, persistent case state, transactional inventory reservations, retries, audit events—became essential to proving the core experience.

Core MVP

  • Structured / free-text request intake
  • Deterministic compatibility and eligibility
  • Parallel inventory + donor matching
  • Shortfall and donor-success ranking
  • Adaptive outreach recommendation
  • Human approval and controlled execution
  • Case state, outcomes, audit trail
  • Blood Weather foundation

Built as beta

  • Document / PDF intake
  • Inventory Copilot / agent investigation
  • SOP retrieval foundation
  • FHIR / HL7-facing interoperability surfaces
  • Additional regional, forecasting and travel features

Left for later

  • End-to-end physical transfer orchestration
  • Full interactive network graph
  • Live external blood-bank integrations
  • Full donation-drive operations
  • Autonomous high-impact decisions
05 / The decision log

The specification changed as the product question became sharper.

These aren't retrospective storytelling pivots. Several major decisions are visible in the project's evolving specification.

  1. The starting bet: rank likely responders.The first mental model was an AI/ML donor-success ranking problem. It later became one component of a larger fulfillment journey.
  2. v1.1 — Add inventory checks in parallel with donor matching.Instead of assuming every request begins with recruiting donors, check nearby compatible stock at the same time and net available units out of the shortfall.
  3. v1.2 — Make the economics workable.The demo cost ceiling tightened from $300 to $250/month. A standing model endpoint was replaced with in-process Cloud Run scoring; a separate retrieval service was replaced with pgvector on the existing database.
  4. v1.3–1.5 — Close the action loop.Human-approved execution, retry-safe event processing, donor notifications, and controlled inventory mutation were promoted into demo scope. Recommendations alone were no longer enough.
  5. v1.6 — Clarify the real differentiation.BloodNet was defined as a layer complementing e-RaktKosh and existing bank systems. Donor notification was explicitly removed as a novelty claim; Gemini investigation became a controlled AI interface, not medical authority.
  6. Later usability iteration — Rethink the front door.Early feedback revealed that the login-first experience obscured the product's purpose. A public regional dashboard helped make the operating model legible before sign-in.

06 / Roads not taken

Not building something is a product decision, too.

The alternative wasn't always a bad idea. Often it was a good idea at the wrong stage, or an attractive shortcut that made the outcome less trustworthy.

not this ↘

Another standalone donor registry

Chosen: coordinate on top of existing registries and inventory sources in the long term. Rebuilding records would duplicate ecosystem infrastructure rather than prove the underserved decision loop.

not this ↘

Blast every compatible donor

Chosen: rank eligible candidates and size an outreach cohort to the actual shortfall. Contacting too few is risky; contacting too many creates fatigue and unnecessary disruption.

not this ↘

Let an AI agent approve reservations or medical eligibility

Chosen: deterministic rules, role-scoped approval, and auditability. Generative reasoning may explain a situation, but it cannot substitute for compatibility rules or an authorized human decision.

not yet ↘

Build the whole transfer logistics operation

Chosen: reservation and coordination first. Dispatch, cold-chain monitoring through transit, receipt, and reconciliation require much deeper operational integration than the MVP could honestly demonstrate.

not yet ↘

Own donation-drive planning end to end

Chosen: make shortage-driven recommendations and send approved regional awareness notifications. Venue scheduling, registrations, attendance, and blood collection operations remain future scope.

instead ↗

Separate deployed microservices and an always-on model endpoint

Chosen: logically separated services in one Cloud Run deployment and local in-process model serving. That kept the MVP easier and cheaper to operate while retaining clear component boundaries.

not yet ↘

Pretend to have live, integrated network data

Chosen: synthetic, explicitly labelled data to test the workflow and its performance. Live third-party bank integration and real-world outcome evidence must come before stronger impact claims.

I wasn't trying to maximize the number of impressive components. I was trying to make the core promise demonstrable.
07 / Model boundaries

AI where ambiguity helps. Rules where safety matters.

The product spans several different kinds of decisions. Treating them all as the same “AI feature” would have been both less useful and harder to trust.

DecisionWho—or what—should make it?
What does this free-text request say?Gemini-assisted extraction, followed by typed validation and a deterministic fallback.
Is this blood / donor eligible?Auditable deterministic compatibility and eligibility rules; not learned or inferred by a language model.
Who is most likely to complete a donation?A request-aware LightGBM ranking model, after hard eligibility filtering.
How many people should we contact?Probability-based cohort sizing, accounting for the remaining shortfall and response likelihood.
Why is an intervention recommended?Constrained AI investigation over allowlisted, read-oriented tools, with provenance and redaction.
Can we reserve / contact / change state?An authorized human approves or rejects; validated deterministic services execute only approved actions.

This separation also shaped the failure modes. If the language model fails, the core constraints remain enforceable. If two requests compete for a scarce unit, transactional reservation prevents double allocation. If events retry, deduplication and idempotency reduce the chance of repeating a consequential action.

And if a recommendation is rejected, it must not quietly mutate operational state. That is a product trust requirement before it is a technical one.

08 / From reactive to proactive

Blood Weather asked a different question: what if we saw the shortfall coming?

Emergency matching responds to today's shortage. Regional forecasting explores whether operators could identify tomorrow's pressure and coordinate earlier.

Blood Weather combines demand/supply signals into short-term regional forecasts, with a baseline forecasting pipeline and a cold-start fallback. Its purpose isn't to decorate a dashboard with predictions; it's to help a regional operator decide whether a specific intervention deserves review.

Case-specific donor swarm

An active request has a defined shortfall. Select a relatively small, eligibility-filtered group of donors likely to help cover it.

Regional donation-drive awareness

A forecast suggests emerging shortage pressure. Prepare an approval-gated recommendation and—if approved—notify relevant hospitals, banks and eligible contactable donors.

The two systems deliberately do not share the same audience or objective. One tries to close an immediate case with minimal unnecessary outreach; the other tries to create awareness around a regional risk. Full campaign logistics are not part of this version.

The data boundary: forecasting and donor-probability models currently use synthetic or simulated histories. Real-world calibration and operational validation are future steps.
09 / An unexpected UX problem

The first screen didn't explain the product. The experience had to change.

After building a role-based system, I found that getting someone inside it wasn't the same as helping them understand it.

In an early usability round, eight people struggled to understand BloodNet from the login-first experience. That feedback exposed a mismatch between the product's complexity and the way a new person encountered it: you had to commit to entering before you could understand why you would want to.

Before

An authentication-led entry point assumed people already understood the actors and the coordination loop.

After

A public regional dashboard and explainer made demand, supply, forecasts, and role journeys visible without first requiring an account.

I then checked the redesigned experience with ten new users. The reported qualitative outcome was better comprehension of BloodNet's purpose. I don't have a controlled, quantified time-to-understand improvement to claim—and I don't want to turn a small usability check into a conversion statistic.

The lesson wasn't to make the landing screen prettier. It was to show why the product mattered before asking someone to enter it.

10 / Results & limits

What I can demonstrate—and what I can't claim yet.

I want a reader to know the difference between a working product loop, a technical benchmark, early user feedback, and real-world healthcare impact.

Working product evidence

The MVP implements the core request → match → shortfall → recommendation → approve → reserve/mobilize → update flow, along with a regional forecasting path. The later beta surfaces explore document intake, AI-assisted investigation and interoperability.

What remains unproven

Real-world fulfillment-rate improvement, model calibration on authentic donor histories, live inventory accuracy, hospital adoption, sustained donor response, and operational impact across a real blood network. The system currently relies on synthetic data for its decision models and demo workflows, and medical/regulatory review would be needed before any clinical deployment.

Metrics I'd prioritize next

  • Request-to-first-actionable-match: did the system accelerate finding a useful option?
  • Fulfillment rate and time-to-resolution: did a recommendation eventually help meet the request?
  • Inventory coverage vs donor mobilization: did we use available supply before creating unnecessary outreach?
  • Donor contact load and response yield: did targeting improve responsiveness without exhausting donors?
  • Override / rejection rate: were recommendations useful and understandable enough for operators to trust?

The performance benchmark measures ranking latency, not prediction quality or patient outcomes.

11 / Reflection

The model was the entry point. The decision system became the product.

I started by asking who might respond to a blood request. The work pushed me into questions that were less glamorous and far more consequential: what inventory can be trusted, how should incomplete supply be handled, when is outreach justified, and who has the authority to act?

Each change in the product made another dependency visible. The ranking model needed reliable data. The matching flow needed durable reservations. Reservations needed authorization. Authorization needed role-aware journeys. And the whole workflow needed an explanation people could understand before they ever signed in.

The version I would work toward next isn't “more AI.” It's better evidence: live source integrations, direct hospital/bank user research, real operational outcomes, and calibration of ranking and forecasting against real-world data. Full transfer orchestration and deeper network analysis become much more useful after those foundations exist.

The most valuable product decision wasn't adding another feature. It was redefining what problem the features were supposed to solve.
